## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the maritime and freight information systems whose movements connect production to demand and whose disruptions rewrite delivered-cost economics: the shipping-market intelligence (the tanker-segment structure where VLCC, Suezmax, and Aframax vessel classes serve different route economics with the dirty-versus-clean product tanker distinction; the dry-bulk ecosystem covering Capesize iron ore and coal flows, Panamax grain and coal, and Supramax minor bulks whose rate indices signal industrial and agricultural trade health; the container-shipping dimension relevant to manufactured goods whose rates carry broader trade-cycle information; the rate-assessment systems where Baltic Exchange indices and broker fixtures provide the daily pricing evidence freight analysts track); the vessel-tracking data discipline (the AIS system literacy where automatic-identification transmissions provide position, speed, and destination data whose gaps, spoofing, and manipulation in sanctioned trade require interpretation skill per the alternative-data companion; the flow-estimation methodology converting vessel movements into cargo volumes through draft reading, capacity tables, and port-call analysis; the floating-storage identification where idle laden tankers become observable inventory buffers whose count and location shift with market structure; the transit-time-and-arrival modeling where loading-to-discharge windows forecast regional supply arrival weeks ahead, the leading-indicator value of maritime data); the chokepoint-and-route analysis (the Strait of Hormuz volume significance where roughly a fifth of global oil transits with disruption scenarios defining tail-risk pricing per the geopolitics section; the Suez and Panama canal mechanics including SUMED pipeline alternatives, transit-queue data, and the draft-restriction dynamics when rainfall fails, with the 2021 Ever Given and 2023-2024 drought episodes as case studies; the Black Sea and Baltic routes whose war-risk insurance, corridor agreements, and attack patterns reshape grain and energy flows per the agricultural trade companion; the Cape routes and Arctic alternatives whose economics shift with fuel prices and ice conditions; the route-economics calculation where distance times cost plus canal fees and insurance enters every delivered-price comparison per the logistics discipline); the port-and-terminal intelligence (the loading-port throughput monitoring where Gulf, Brazilian, Black Sea, and Middle East terminal activity reveals export pace ahead of official statistics; the discharge-port congestion tracking where queue lengths at Chinese and Asian terminals signal demand health and demurrage exposure; the terminal-outage and weather monitoring where hurricanes, strikes, and maintenance interrupt flows with balance-sheet consequences; the infrastructure-capacity literacy where berth availability, storage connectivity, and pipeline interfaces bound export capability per the supply-chain companion); the freight-cost transmission analysis (the delivered-price-arbitrage integration where freight movements open and close origin-competition windows in grain, LNG, and crude flows per the trade-flow discipline; the demurrage-and-waiting-cost economics where congestion converts time into money affecting charter decisions and cargo pricing; the bunker-fuel linkage connecting energy prices into freight costs with the low-sulfur transition economics documented in recent history; the freight-forward-curve literacy where FFAs and route futures express freight views directly, connecting maritime intelligence to tradeable instruments per the derivatives companion); the sanctions-and-shadow-fleet tracking (the dark-fleet phenomenon where aging tankers with opaque ownership and disabled AIS move sanctioned crude whose volume estimation challenges conventional tracking; the ship-to-ship-transfer identification where cargo laundering occurs at anchorage zones requiring pattern recognition in tracking data; the insurance-and-services architecture whose Western dominance creates enforcement leverage points whose usage data reveals compliance levels; the sanctions-efficacy assessment where flow data measures whether restrictions redirect versus reduce trade, informing geopolitical scenarios); and the maritime-analysis craft (the flow-balance integration where vessel movements reconcile against production, stocks, and consumption estimates, exposing data inconsistencies that reveal true supply-demand states; the seasonal-flow patterns where grain export surges, winter LNG demand, and summer crude movements create predictable vessel-demand cycles affecting freight and delivered costs; the disruption-scenario preparation where chokepoint, canal, and port events receive pre-analyzed balance and rerouting impacts per the scenario discipline; the cross-source verification where AIS data, port reports, broker assessments, and official statistics triangulate into reliable flow intelligence per the redundancy practice) together with the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the FOB-only analyst whose price views never incorporate the freight component of delivered costs, remediated by the arbitrage integration discipline; the AIS-literalist who takes transmitted destinations and positions at face value including in sanctioned and spoofed trades, remediated by the tracking-skepticism skills; the chokepoint-hypervisor who reprices every regional incident as route closure against rerouting and insurance realities, remediated by the scenario-preparation calibration; the congestion-ignorer whose arrival forecasts omit queue and discharge delays, remediated by the port-intelligence monitoring; the shadow-flow denier whose sanctioned-market balances count only visible vessels, remediated by the dark-fleet estimation methods; and the freight-static modeler whose annual delivered-cost assumptions survive rate doubling uncorrected, remediated by the cost-transmission integration with detection methods as the diagnostic.
