## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the logistics economics that connect production regions to demand centers and decouple local prices from world prices: the logistics system architecture (the origin-to-destination chain spanning farm or mine gate, inland transport, elevation and storage, port handling, ocean voyage, and destination distribution with each node adding cost, time, and congestion risk; the modal comparison where rail, truck, barge, and pipeline carry different cost-speed-capacity economics, the barge-versus-rail competition on US grain corridors as case study; the chokepoint geography where Panama and Suez canals, the Bosporus, Black Sea ports, and key rail bridges concentrate systemic risk; the infrastructure-capacity constraints where export-elevator throughput and rail-car availability cap shipments regardless of supply size); the freight economics (the ocean-freight market structure spanning dry bulk with its Capesize-Panamax-Supramax vessel classes, container shipping with alliance capacity management, and tanker markets for crude and products; the freight-rate systems including the Baltic Exchange indices and grain-route quotes whose movements enter delivered-cost arbitrage; the vessel-supply dynamics where shipbuilding cycles run three-to-four years creating their own cobweb in shipping capacity; the bunker-fuel cost transmission linking energy prices into freight; the demurrage and port-congestion economics where waiting time becomes cost during disruptions); the trade-flow analysis (the export-competition mapping where Brazilian, US, and Argentine corn and soybeans contest the same import demand with seasonal handoff windows governed by harvest calendars and freight; the delivered-price-arbitrage calculation combining FOB origin price, freight, insurance, and quality adjustments into CIF comparison, the analyst's core logistics computation; the trade-route disruption history where Black Sea war corridors, Panama drought draft limits, and Suez blockages rewired flows with persistent cost effects; the cabotage and inland-regulation layer including Jones Act effects on US domestic shipping); the storage and handling network (the country-elevator and terminal-elevator system whose capacity utilization governs basis behavior; the port-stock visibility where destination-country inventory piles signal import demand saturation or restocking need; the storage-economics integration where facility scarcity during harvest compresses basis and where export-logistics costs enter the full-carry arithmetic of the price-formation section; the quality-preservation logistics including drying, aeration, and handling loss economics); the energy and metals logistics (the pipeline-network economics where takeaway capacity from production basins creates regional price differentials including the famous basis blowouts when pipelines fill; the LNG-carrier fleet dynamics where vessel availability and charter rates gate the gas arbitrage of the energy section; the bulk-metals shipping where Capesize rates move iron ore and coal economics; the container-manufacturing supply chains where component flows govern industrial demand visibility); the logistics-data ecosystem (the freight-rate publication systems and assessment methodologies; the vessel-tracking data from AIS whose aggregation into flow estimates the alternative-data course details; the port-throughput and rail-carload statistics as weekly activity indicators; the basis-quote publication networks from cash-market reporting; the congestion-and-wait-time indicators during disruptions); and the supply-chain-risk framework (the single-point-of-failure analysis identifying where physical concentration creates price risk; the just-in-time versus resilience inventory shift changing global stock-holding behavior after pandemic disruptions; the sanctions-and-compliance logistics where shadow fleets and price caps create two-tier markets; the climate-logistics exposure where low-water rivers and heat-limited rail operations add weather sensitivity to transport itself) together with the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the teleporting-commodity modeler whose world balances ignore freight entirely until a basis blowout surprises the position, remedied by the delivered-price discipline; the FOB-CIF confuser who compares quotes across terms without the freight bridge, remedied by the arbitrage-calculation workflow; the chokepoint-panicker who prices every canal delay as a supply shock without stock and rerouting context, remedied by the disruption-history calibration; the capacity-static analyst who treats elevator, pipeline, and vessel capacity as fixed during congestion eras, remedied by the utilization monitoring; the freight-ignored spread trader whose inter-market spread view omits the freight component of the price gap, remedied by the component decomposition; and the shadow-blind sanction analyst who counts official flows only in sanctioned markets, remedied by the two-tier-market literacy with detection methods as the diagnostic.
