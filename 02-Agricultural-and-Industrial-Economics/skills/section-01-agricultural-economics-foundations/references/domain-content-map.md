## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the economic structure of agricultural production that governs how crop and livestock supply responds to price signals: the sector architecture (the farm-production core where millions of independent operating units make planting and herd decisions under biological constraints and price uncertainty; the input-supply industries including seed, fertilizer, crop protection, feed, and machinery whose cost movements transmit into production economics; the processing-and-marketing chain where grain moves through elevators, crushers, feeders, and packers with the value-added distribution studied through the marketing-margin framework; the sector accounts where national agricultural statistics track production, income, and land values as the macro frame for micro analysis); the production-economics core (the production-function view where yields respond to land, labor, capital, and management with diminishing returns; the cost-structure anatomy separating variable costs that adjust within a season from fixed costs and land rents that adjust across seasons, the distinction that explains why farmers keep producing at prices below total cost; the supply-response asymmetry where price increases trigger area and input expansion but price declines meet acreage stickiness because of sunk commitments and land-use inertia; the risk-management reality where crop insurance, forward contracting, and futures hedging shape planting decisions independently of expected price, a distortion of textbook supply logic that analysts must model explicitly); the land system (the farmland-value determinants where capitalized returns, interest rates, and non-farm demand interact, connecting agricultural economics to the macro rate environment of the companion course; the acreage-allocation competition where corn, soybeans, wheat, and cotton bid against each other for the same acres with the price-ratio decision rules farmers actually use; the crop-rotation constraints where agronomic requirements limit pure price response; the land-use-margin dynamics where high prices bring marginal land into production with yield consequences); the farm-decision calendar (the planting-decision window where acreage intentions form months before harvest with the input-purchase commitments that lock costs early; the growing-season information flow where condition ratings and weather accumulate into yield expectations; the harvest-and-marketing decisions where storage, sell, or forward choices respond to basis and carry per the storage economics of later sections; the program-enrollment cycles where government support participation shapes baseline acreage per the policy section); the global-structure layer (the production-geography mapping where export surpluses concentrate in the Americas and Black Sea while demand growth concentrates in Asia, the structural flow pattern underlying all trade analysis; the producer-economics comparison where Brazilian frontier expansion, US yield leadership, and Argentine policy distortion create different supply curves for the same crop; the development-transition dynamics where rising incomes shift demand from staple grains toward protein and vegetables, the demand-side engine of the livestock complex); the data-ecosystem introduction (the official-statistics system whose census, survey, and estimate products this course's balance-sheet analysis consumes; the private-estimate ecosystem whose yield and acreage guesses trade ahead of official numbers; the satellite-and-alternative-data entrants whose crop-monitoring capabilities the companion alternative-data course details); and the analyst's posture (the physical-literacy requirement where understanding planting windows, growth stages, and harvest logistics separates informed analysis from narrative trading; the biological-time-horizon discipline where supply responds on crop and herd cycles rather than continuously) together with the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the price-cost-blind analyst who cannot explain why acreage holds up below break-even, remedied by the fixed-cost and sunk-commitment economics; the rotation-ignorer who models soybean acreage response to soybean prices alone, remedied by the multi-crop allocation competition framework; the insurance-distortion denier who reads planted acreage as pure price signal, remedied by the risk-management integration; the hemisphere-confuser who treats southern-hemisphere crops as same-season supply, remedied by the global-calendar literacy; and the farmland-rate disconnector who ignores how interest rates capitalize into land costs and acreage decisions, remedied by the land-system macro linkage with detection methods as the diagnostic.
