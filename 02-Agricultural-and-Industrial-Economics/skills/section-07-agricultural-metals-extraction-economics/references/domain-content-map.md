## Unit 1 — Unit 1

**Scope:** Covers the metals complex whose extreme capital intensity and decade-scale supply response create the longest production cycles in fundamental analysis: the metals taxonomy (the precious-metals system where gold and silver price off monetary, portfolio, and industrial demand with mine supply playing a secondary role, connecting directly to the macro course's rate and inflation channels; the base-metals group of copper, aluminum, zinc, nickel, and lead whose demand tracks construction, manufacturing, and electrification activity; the ferrous complex of iron ore and steel whose China-centered flows dominate global bulk commodity trade; the battery-and-minor-metals layer including lithium, cobalt, and rare earths whose energy-transition demand created new market structures); the extraction economics core (the orebody-to-market chain spanning exploration, discovery, feasibility, financing, construction, operation, and closure with the ten-to-fifteen-year timelines that make supply response glacial; the grade concept and its decline dynamics where falling ore grades raise energy and capital intensity per unit metal, a structural cost escalator; the cost-curve analysis with cash costs versus all-in sustaining costs and the marginal-mine price-setting logic; the by-product economics where copper mines yielding molybdenum or gold mines yielding silver respond to combined revenue, distorting single-metal supply logic; the reserve-and-resource classification systems governing what reported inventory of ore actually means); the investment-cycle dynamics (the exploration-cycle response where discovery investment follows prices with multi-year lags and discovery rates decline structurally; the capital-discipline eras where shareholder pressure replaced growth-at-any-cost mining finance, altering supply response; the project-pipeline analysis tracking announced expansions and their realistic delivery timelines against company statements; the state-and-jurisdiction dimension where resource nationalism, permitting delays, and political risk reshape supply geography; the scrap-and-recycling supply stream whose price elasticity differs fundamentally from mine supply, the secondary-supply buffer); the smelting and refining layer (the treatment-and-refining-charge system where smelter margins expressed as TC-RCs signal concentrate surplus or scarcity before metal prices move, the metals complex's unique leading indicator; the capacity-geography where Chinese smelting expansion reshaped global flows; the exchange-deliverable versus chemical-grade quality distinctions governing LME and COMEX contract mechanics); the demand systems (the industrial-demand elasticity where construction, manufacturing, automotive, and grid investment drive base metals with China's share making its policy cycle the dominant demand variable; the electrification-demand thesis where EV, grid, and renewable intensity per unit raises copper and battery-metal demand trajectories; the precious-metal demand anatomy spanning investment, central-bank reserve purchases, jewelry with cultural price sensitivity, and industrial use; the substitution-and-thrifting dynamics where high prices engineer metal out of products over multi-year horizons); the market-structure layer (the LME warehouse-system mechanics whose queue dynamics and cancellation ratios signal physical tightness; the exchange-versus-OTC duality and the 2022 nickel episode as case study in market-structure failure; the stock-visibility problem where off-warrant and shadow inventories hide true supply buffers; the contango-carry trade where financing deals absorb surplus metal, distorting curve interpretation); the price-formation synthesis (the cost-support concept where cash-cost curves set soft floors through high-cost supply curtailment rather than hard price floors; the inventory-price relationship parallel to agriculture's stocks-to-use logic but with above-ground stocks dwarfing annual mine supply for some metals; the dollar-denomination effect where the inverse currency relationship imports macro into every metal price; the positioning-and-financialization intensity where metals trade as macro proxies amplifying fundamental moves) together with the section anti-patterns — the failure library: the ag-analogy importer who expects mine supply to respond within a season like acreage, remedied by the decade-cycle timeline discipline; the cost-floor-literalist who treats cash costs as hard price floors ignoring that high-cost supply can lose money for years, remedied by the soft-support curtailment mechanics; the by-product-blind forecaster who models zinc supply from zinc prices when much zinc is a lead or copper byproduct, remedied by the combined-revenue logic; the TC-RC illiterate who misses concentrate-market signals available months before metal-price effects, remedied by the smelting-margin monitoring; the visible-stock-only analyst whose balance omits shadow inventories, remedied by the stock-visibility discipline; and the China-demand-monolith who models Chinese metal demand as one number instead of construction-manufacturing-grid-export components, remedied by the demand-component decomposition with detection methods as the diagnostic.
